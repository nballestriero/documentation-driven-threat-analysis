## Why this source is central

The source maps the boundary between requirements engineering and software architecture:

```text
requirements specification
-> architectural analysis and concern identification
-> candidate architectural decisions
-> architectural elements, relations and views
-> architecture evaluation
```

It complements SRC-0033, which establishes broad document-to-candidate-artifact automation. SRC-0034
shows that the later requirements-to-architecture transition is not a deterministic continuation of
that automation. It requires alternatives, quality-attribute trade-offs, architectural knowledge,
manual refinement and evaluation.

The source is historical and does not describe the 2026 state of tools or LLM support. Its enduring
value is the evidence that transformation rules are narrow, tacit knowledge remains central,
traceability is exceptional and generated architecture requires explicit evaluation.

## Four reading questions

### 1. Research problem and contribution

Software architecture represents high-level elements, their externally visible properties and their
relations. The derivation problem moves from stakeholder needs and requirements in the problem space
to architectural choices in the solution space.

The activity contains three distinct responsibilities:

```text
understand the problem
-> find an architectural solution
-> evaluate the proposed solution
```

Application-domain forces and quality attributes can conflict. Patterns, styles and alternatives may
satisfy stakeholder goals to different degrees. The mapping study therefore asks which methods have
been proposed for deriving architectural models from requirements specifications.

The study classifies methods using four NIMSAD dimensions:

1. context;
2. benefits to users;
3. method content;
4. validation.

Its search combines IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, ScienceDirect and SpringerLink, forward
snowballing and expert suggestions without a publication-date restriction.

The selection flow is reported as:

```text
2575 initial candidates
-> 78 relevant studies before final assessment
-> 39 selected primary studies
```

The protocol was reviewed by three external reviewers, piloted and checked with Fabbri's checklist.
The quality gate combines contribution alignment, method formalization, architectural description,
evaluation, reported limitations, venue ranking and citation information.

#### Reporting inconsistency

Table 6 cannot be reconciled arithmetically. In the ScienceDirect column, 203 inclusions minus the
listed exclusions yields eight studies, but the table reports nine. The displayed column totals sum
to 39 only by using that nine. This discrepancy is preserved rather than corrected.

### 2. Starting artifacts and generated representations

All 39 studies address functional requirements. Thirty also address non-functional requirements:

| NFR type | Studies among the 30 |
|---|---:|
| Product NFR | 28 |
| Organizational NFR | 9 |
| External NFR | 1 |

The categories overlap. External requirements, including business and environmental forces, are
almost absent.

Input representations are:

| Starting representation | Studies |
|---|---:|
| Textual requirements | 20 |
| Goal-oriented model | 8 |
| Unspecified model | 6 |
| Feature model | 2 |
| Problem frames | 2 |
| Sequence diagram | 1 |

Among the 20 textual-input studies:

- nine structure requirements as use cases;
- four construct intermediate models;
- two cluster requirements to identify architectural modules;
- five do not describe the input structure sufficiently.

The documentation-first classification is:

```text
requirements_primary_with_model_mediation
```

Text is the largest input group, but many methods require a structured model or an architect-defined
set of critical architectural requirements before derivation begins.

#### Architectural views

Nineteen studies do not explicitly name their views. The others use 28 view names and 80 view
instances. The same name can express different semantics and different names can express the same
purpose.

The authors normalize the views through Kruchten's 4+1 model. This enables comparison but does not
prove semantic equivalence. Variability, quality, customer, crosscutting, macro and other useful views
remain partly or wholly outside the selected baseline.

#### Architecture description languages

```text
13 standard ADL, including UML, SysML or AADL
13 non-standard notation
13 notation not identified
```

Output language and view semantics therefore require explicit provenance.

### 3. Traceability, change, automation and human role

The real automation boundary is:

```text
specific requirement representation
+ transformation rule or heuristic
-> specific candidate architectural model
-> manual analysis and improvement
```

The reported support is:

| Support | Studies |
|---|---:|
| Partially automated | 18, 46.1% |
| Not automated | 8, 20.5% |
| Tool not mentioned | 13, 33.3% |
| Fully automated | 0 |

All reported architectural decisions depend on architects' experience and intuition, including in
approaches with supporting tools. The quality of produced artifacts is often judged subjectively or
not evaluated.

The claimed benefits are:

| Benefit | Studies |
|---|---:|
| Architecture derivation | 33 |
| Requirements understanding | 12 |
| Decision support | 12 |
| Architectural-knowledge reuse | 6 |
| Improved modularization | 2 |
| Requirements-to-architecture traceability | 1 |

Traceability is therefore not an established field-wide capability.

Only eight studies cover problem understanding, solution synthesis and solution evaluation together.
Many methods are described too coarsely for replication.

The source does not evaluate maintained synchronization, stale detection, change-impact analysis or
automatic propagation after requirement changes. It discusses architectural knowledge vaporization:
decision rationale can disappear during evolution even when the current structure remains visible.

Human responsibilities remain:

- identify architecturally significant requirements;
- interpret NFRs and domain constraints;
- generate and compare alternatives;
- select patterns, styles and tactics;
- resolve trade-offs;
- correct generated elements and views;
- validate requirements satisfaction;
- maintain decisions and rationale after change.

### 4. Findings, limitations and relationship with DDTA

#### Method limitations

Thirty-two studies, 82 percent, do not clearly report limitations. The limitations that are reported
include immaturity, lack of tool support, weak requirements understanding, underspecified decisions,
artifact inconsistency, semantic loss and missing traceability.

#### Evaluation evidence

```text
20 claimed case studies
9 examples
3 experiments
7 without evaluation
```

The authors observe that many claimed case studies are illustrative examples or omit hypotheses,
data collection and analysis. Twenty-seven evaluation approaches are academic, eight have industrial
roots and two include both academic and industrial cases.

#### Requirements-satisfaction validation

Only four studies use or suggest ATAM. Other named or scenario-based techniques appear, but 21
studies, 53.8 percent, do not state how the resulting architecture should be evaluated against the
requirements specification.

```text
architecture generated
!= requirements satisfied
!= alternatives compared
!= trade-offs justified
!= architecture validated
```

#### Mapping-study limitations

The authors acknowledge risks from terminology diversity, unavailable papers, search-engine changes,
subjective interpretation and data extraction. Mitigations include external reviewers, pilot search,
snowballing, expert suggestions, test-retest checks, quality scoring, a checklist and structured data
extraction.

## Research roadmap

The eight proposed directions are:

1. domain-specific approaches;
2. wider NFR coverage, especially external NFRs;
3. explicit reporting of method limitations;
4. standards or common terminology;
5. externalization of tacit knowledge;
6. stronger tool and decision support;
7. stronger qualitative and quantitative evaluation;
8. explicit validation of requirements satisfaction.

## Core definitions

### Architecturally significant requirement

A functional or non-functional requirement whose satisfaction materially constrains architectural
elements, relations, patterns, styles, tactics or deployment choices.

### Architectural synthesis

The construction and comparison of candidate architectural solutions after the problem and relevant
concerns have been understood.

### Architectural evaluation

Assessment of whether and to what degree a candidate architecture satisfies requirements and quality
attributes, including the consequences of trade-offs.

### Tacit architectural knowledge

Experience, intuition, heuristics and context used by an architect but not explicitly represented in
the derivation method or artifacts.

### Architectural knowledge vaporization

Loss of design-decision knowledge and rationale during architecture evolution.

## Traceability, change, automation and human role

### Traceability present

One primary study reports requirements-to-architecture traceability as a benefit. The wider corpus
does not establish maintained assertion-to-decision-to-element links.

### Change

Longitudinal synchronization and stale detection are not evaluated. The paper identifies loss of
decision knowledge during evolution as a problem.

### Automation

Partial, representation-specific rule transformations exist. No complete automated derivation
approach is identified.

### Human review

Architects must supply interpretation, alternatives, trade-offs, decisions, corrections and
evaluation. Generated views remain candidates until reviewed.

## Documentation-first classification

### Classification

`requirements_primary_with_model_mediation`

Requirements specifications are the main source, but structured use cases, goal models, feature
models, problem frames, intermediate models and architect-selected critical requirements frequently
mediate the transition.

### Nine documentation-first fields

1. Input and quality: functional requirements dominate; NFR and external-context coverage is uneven.
2. Compatibility: requirements-primary but representation-specific.
3. Intermediate representation: use cases, goals, feature models, problem frames, clusters, concerns and architectural decisions.
4. Procedure: understand, synthesize alternatives, decide, project views, evaluate and refine.
5. Output: candidate elements, relations, views and architectural models.
6. Provenance, uncertainty, review: tacit knowledge dominates and maintained traceability is rare.
7. Evolution/change: decision vaporization is recognized; synchronization is not evaluated.
8. Evaluation/evidence: 39 heterogeneous studies with weak rigorous empirical evidence.
9. Thesis use: requirements-to-architecture boundary and decision-provenance requirements.

## Directly supported

- Textual and model-based requirements can be inputs to architectural derivation.
- Functional requirements and NFRs influence architectural choices.
- Representation-specific rules can generate candidate architectural models.
- Multiple views are necessary for complex architectures.
- Partial automation still requires manual architectural analysis and improvement.
- Architectural decisions depend strongly on experience and intuition.
- Requirements-to-architecture traceability is rarely an explicit supported benefit.
- Standard terminology and ADL usage are inconsistent.
- More than half of the methods lack explicit requirements-satisfaction evaluation.
- Strong empirical and industrial validation is limited.

## Not supported

- Requirements determine one architecture.
- Generated architectures are complete or correct.
- Architectural decisions are normally explicit and traceable.
- One view or ADL is sufficient for every concern.
- 4+1 normalization preserves all original semantics.
- The methods handle missing or contradictory requirements reliably.
- Architecture and requirements remain synchronized after change.
- Requirements satisfaction is normally demonstrated.
- The corpus describes modern LLM-based architecture synthesis.
- The mapping study establishes current 2026 tool maturity.

## Eight DDTA evidence questions

1. Documents sufficient without code: requirements can initiate derivation; architecture decisions still require expert knowledge.
2. Neutral intermediate representation: not defined; inputs and architectural outputs are heterogeneous.
3. Extracted versus inferred: not standardized; architectural choices are largely tacit.
4. Method as overlay: transformations are specific to requirement and architecture representations.
5. Provenance, confidence, review: traceability is rare and confidence semantics are absent.
6. Documentation change: not evaluated; decision knowledge can vaporize during evolution.
7. Later code evidence: outside the main derivation scope; architecture precedes detailed design and implementation.
8. Measurement: examples and weak case studies dominate; explicit output validation is often absent.

## Relationship with Documentation-Driven Threat Analysis

### Base Analysis implications

DDTA should preserve enough evidence to support architectural reasoning:

- actors, goals, assets and responsibilities;
- functional behavior and interactions;
- data and control flows;
- functional and non-functional requirements;
- organizational, external and regulatory constraints;
- quality attributes and conflicts;
- domain assumptions and technologies;
- source provenance, uncertainty and review state.

### Requirements-to-architecture plugin implications

A plugin should declare:

- accepted requirement representations;
- required NFR, domain and context information;
- architecturally significant requirement criteria;
- intermediate concern and decision schemas;
- transformation rules and heuristics;
- external knowledge sources;
- human decision points;
- alternative-generation and comparison strategy;
- supported views and ADL versions;
- traceability granularity;
- semantic-loss risks;
- evaluation method and satisfaction criteria;
- change-impact, stale-detection and regeneration behavior.

### Candidate architecture lifecycle

```text
requirement evidence accepted
-> architecturally significant concern identified
-> alternatives generated
-> decision pending
-> decision accepted | rejected | deferred
-> candidate elements and views produced
-> architecture evaluation pending
-> accepted | corrected | rejected
-> stale after requirement, rationale or transformer change
-> re-evaluated and re-reviewed
```

DDTA must not promote a generated component, relation or view to canonical architecture without an
explicit decision and evaluation record.

## Candidate thesis claims

Supported directly or conditionally:

- Requirements-to-architecture derivation is a decision process, not only a model transformation.
- Generated architecture elements require links to source requirements and decision rationale.
- Quality-attribute conflicts require alternatives and explicit trade-off evidence.
- Architectural views should be typed projections with declared semantics.
- Partial automation can assist derivation but does not replace architectural evaluation.
- Change governance must preserve decisions and rationale to prevent knowledge vaporization.

Not supported:

- DDTA can derive a correct architecture deterministically from documentation.
- Every requirement has one direct architecture mapping.
- More detailed output implies better requirements satisfaction.
- A standard ADL automatically solves semantic interoperability.
- A generated component graph is sufficient architecture evidence.
- Historical absence of full automation proves current LLM systems cannot assist.

## Research gap left open

The study maps conventional requirements-to-architecture methods but predates modern generative LLM
support. It does not establish how an LLM should preserve assertion-level provenance, separate
extracted facts from design choices, generate and compare alternatives, justify trade-offs, maintain
rationale after change or evaluate architecture satisfaction reproducibly.

The next source should examine requirements-to-UML or requirements-to-model generation with modern
large language models.
