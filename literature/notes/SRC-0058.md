## Four reading questions

### 1. Research problem and contribution

EARS addresses common defects in unconstrained natural-language stakeholder requirements by imposing a small syntactic ruleset. It is explicitly an authoring/syntax technique, not a domain classifier. The paper defines a generic requirement form and five base types based on preconditions/triggers/response.

### 2. Starting artifacts and system representation

The case starts from regulatory text and an engineering task to extract high-level aero-engine control-system requirements. EARS restructures requirement statements into a fixed clause order with optional preconditions and trigger, a system name and one or more system responses. The paper does not claim to derive the correct system semantics from the syntax itself.

### 3. Traceability, change, automation and human role

Application in the reported case is human-led. Rewording and reclassification occurred over iterations as the ruleset evolved. There is no project-authority or BA model; EARS constrains prose.

### 4. Findings, limitations and relationship with DDTA / overlay

The paper reports qualitative and quantitative improvements in a small case, but the evidence is limited and partly subjective. Its strongest DDTA relevance is negative/structural: better syntax can improve readability and detect some defects, but cannot answer whether the author chose the right semantic responsibility in the first place.

## Research method and corpus

Lightweight syntax design plus application to an airworthiness-regulation extraction case; qualitative and quantitative comparison.

## Contribution

Five simple natural-language requirement patterns and a generic clause order.

## Findings

- A small syntax can reduce ambiguity/complexity in requirement statements.
- The technique targets high-level textual requirements and preserves natural language.
- Syntax and semantic classification are separate concerns.

## Limitations stated by the authors / accessible source

- Small/specialised case and subjective quality assessment.
- Does not determine domain semantics or validate project authority.
- Later EARS variants/practice exceed the 2009 evidence.

## Relationship with Documentation-Driven Threat Analysis

### Directly supported propositions

- Requirement syntax can be constrained without replacing natural language.
- A syntactic pattern cannot by itself validate the semantic class of a requirement.

### Tensions or contradictions

- Using a clean EARS sentence around a misclassified capability could make an incorrect meaning look more precise.

### Research gap left open

Upstream semantic recognition and authority validation.

### Overlay-method implications

Potential final authoring/checking layer after project meaning is established.
