## Four reading questions

### 1. Research problem and contribution

The paper tackles requirements/problem-level reuse rather than limiting reuse to implementation/design patterns. It develops a domain theory of grounded domains, meta-domains and generic tasks, plus reusable OSM/ISM models and recognition guidance. It also attaches design rationale so a generic problem model can expose downstream design issues without equating the problem model with the solution.

### 2. Starting artifacts and system representation

The method begins with requirements for a new application, partitions the application, identifies goals, and analyses objects, structures, agents and state transitions. A walkthrough then seeks generic models using goodness-of-fit. Structural objects and primary state transitions are especially discriminating; secondary states such as reservation are explicitly described as weak discriminators because they occur in many domains.

### 3. Traceability, change, automation and human role

Recognition is presented as an analyst-guided process. The paper reports reuse experience and earlier experiments showing a danger of novice 'copy-cat' transfer when models are reused without sufficient reasoning. This is an important negative result for DDTA: reusable models can amplify error if accepted uncritically. The source does not provide a documentation-provenance or authority workflow.

### 4. Findings, limitations and relationship with DDTA / overlay

The paper reports a reuse case and discusses experimental experience, but its conclusion is deliberately qualified. It records criticism about repository size, integration with conventional processes, collaborative-system modelling and mappings from requirements-level domain models to solution patterns. It calls for industrial assessment and continued empirical refinement. The paper therefore supports recognition heuristics and explicit discriminators while directly warning against uncritical pattern transfer.

## Research method and corpus

Domain theory + reusable model library + walkthrough recognition method + case study and reported experimental/experience evidence.

## Contribution

Problem/domain reuse method with recognition heuristics, generic semantic models and linked design rationale.

## Findings

- Problem-domain abstractions can be organised and reused across application domains.
- Structure plus state transitions are stronger discriminators than common secondary properties/states.
- Recognition is difficult because abstractions are rarely explicit in requirements.
- Novice copy-and-specialise reuse produced misapplication errors in reported studies.

## Limitations stated by the authors / accessible source

- Library coverage/completeness and repository cost remain issues.
- Collaborative systems and model aggregation need more work.
- Industrial utility needed further assessment.
- Linking requirements-level models to solution patterns remained incomplete.

## Relationship with Documentation-Driven Threat Analysis

### Directly supported propositions

- A recognition walkthrough can move from goals and domain structure toward candidate reusable models.
- Not all attributes are equally discriminating; common properties can create false similarity.
- Uncritical reuse can cause copy-cat errors.

### Tensions or contradictions

- The source eventually links problem models to design rationale/solutions; DDTA must maintain a stronger authority boundary.
- Its models depend on analyst-created semantic representations rather than extraction from governed documentation.

### Research gap left open

Provenance-aware acquisition of the semantic facts that feed recognition and controlled governance of accepted/rejected matches.

### Overlay-method implications

DDTA should preserve mismatches and review evidence, and should explicitly block automatic transfer from a pattern/model into governed documentation or BA.

## Candidate thesis claims

- Pattern-like reuse needs discriminators and review, not mere naming similarity.
- A reusable model may be useful even when it is not authoritative or exhaustive.
- Failure modes of reuse include overfitting and uncritical transfer.
