## Four reading questions

### 1. Research problem and contribution

The paper investigates whether a pretrained BERT-based requirements classifier can identify
requirement fragments that are irrelevant for requirement-to-code trace-link recovery. Existing
methods compare requirements and source code but often process text that describes users,
quality constraints or other content that is not directly mappable to classes and methods. Such
content can create false-positive candidate links.

The authors combine NoRBERT with the fine-grained FTLR method. They create a new manually
labeled dataset of requirement elements and run two empirical studies: one evaluates
classification on unseen projects; the second evaluates whether classification-based filtering
improves FTLR. The code, dataset references and further results are published as supplementary
material.

### 2. Starting artifacts and system representation

The study starts from natural-language requirements and source-code artifacts in five benchmark
projects: eTour, iTrust, SMOS, eAnci and LibEST. The requirements comprise templated use cases,
flows of events and ordinary textual requirements. SMOS and eAnci are translated from Italian
to English because the evaluated NoRBERT setup accepts English input.

Requirements are split into elements and manually labeled for functional aspects (`F`),
user-related content (`UR`) and the functional concerns Function, Behavior and Data. The new
traceability subset contains 447 requirements and 3,284 requirement elements; adding the PNFR
training data yields 691 requirements and 3,908 elements.

The method does not derive an architecture, data-flow model or security model. It filters text
before matching requirement elements to source-code elements. Its findings apply to candidate
link recovery, not to document-to-Base-Analysis transformation.

### 3. Traceability, change, automation and human role

NoRBERT classifies each requirement element. FTLR can then remove elements without functional
aspects, remove user-related elements, or combine both filters before similarity calculation and
aggregation. The paper explicitly distinguishes an element without functional aspects from a
non-functional requirement: these are not equivalent categories.

The classification evaluation follows a leave-one-project-out-like strategy. Each target project
is treated as unseen; NoRBERT is trained on the remaining projects plus PNFR, except that the
user-related class lacks PNFR labels. Two annotators independently create the gold standard and
resolve disagreements through discussion.

Automation does not eliminate human dependence. The gold standard is human-labeled, per-project
threshold optimization depends on known links and represents an upper bound, and severe class
imbalance can create unusable false-positive rates. In LibEST, the user-related classifier
recovers four of five positive elements but has only 1.7 percent precision.

### 4. Findings, limitations and relationship with DDTA / overlay

The paper reports a combined weighted F1 of 84 percent for functional and user-related aspects.
The detailed table reports weighted F1 values around 89.8 percent for functional aspects and
81.8 percent for user-related content under the best displayed configurations. Performance is
substantially worse for some concern classes and projects, showing that one headline score does
not describe all downstream error risks.

Using the automated functional-aspect filter improves the best average FTLR F1 with original
thresholds from 34.5 to 35.3 percent and with per-project optimized thresholds from 37.8 to
38.8 percent; the latter difference is statistically significant in the reported Wilcoxon test.
The user-related filter reduces average performance because recall falls. A template filter
performs slightly better than the automated functional filter, but is applicable only to
templated projects. Combining template and functional filters reaches 40.7 percent average F1
under optimized thresholds.

Against selected unsupervised baselines and competing methods, the best filtered FTLR variant
achieves 37.1 percent average F1 and 40.6 percent F2 under per-project optimized thresholds.
The strongest method still changes by project: on SMOS, LSI-based methods outperform FTLR.

For DDTA, this source supports task-specific preprocessing, component-level evaluation and
unseen-project testing. It does not support indiscriminate removal of non-functional or
user-related text: security, trust-boundary and misuse evidence may reside precisely in those
fragments. Filtering semantics must therefore be declared by each overlay and every discarded
fragment must remain traceable.

## Research method and corpus

- Peer-reviewed RE 2024 research paper.
- Five requirement-to-code benchmark projects.
- 447 requirements and 3,284 newly labeled requirement elements.
- Combined with PNFR: 691 requirements and 3,908 elements.
- Labels: functional aspect, user-related, Function, Behavior and Data.
- Two annotators: one author and one computer-science master's student.
- Krippendorff's alpha ranges from 0.742 to 0.905 by label.
- Leave-one-project-out-like classification evaluation.
- BERTBASE and BERTLARGE configurations, 10 or 16 epochs, with or without oversampling.
- TLR evaluation with original and per-project optimized thresholds.
- Metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, F1, F2 and MAP.
- Comparison methods: VSM, LSI, TAROT, COMET and FTLR variants.
- Wilcoxon signed-rank test for the reported automated-filter F1 improvement.

## Contribution

- A labeled dataset of requirement elements for traceability benchmarks.
- An empirical assessment of pretrained BERT classification on unseen projects.
- Integration of classification as a preprocessing filter in FTLR.
- Comparison of automatic, gold-standard and template-based filters.
- Public source code and further result files.

## Findings

- Functional and user-related fragments can be classified with promising aggregate performance.
- Project and class imbalance materially affect precision, recall and F1.
- The functional-aspect filter improves FTLR modestly but significantly in the reported setup.
- The user-related filter can damage recall and average F1.
- Template and automatic filters are complementary on projects where both apply.
- The strongest method and configuration remain dataset-dependent.
- Gold-standard and optimized-threshold results are upper bounds, not deployable estimates.
- The reported absolute average F1 remains about 0.4 even for the best configuration.

## Limitations stated by the authors

- The benchmark projects may not represent real industrial projects.
- Several requirements were written by students.
- The datasets are biased toward functional or non-functional content and are imbalanced.
- The manually constructed gold standard may be influenced by the intended approach.
- Machine translation of SMOS and eAnci can introduce classification errors.
- The second experiment uses a limited set of mostly academic or student projects.
- Fully representative industrial datasets with established trace-link ground truth are unavailable.
- The filtering result has not been generalized to other TLR methods.

## Additional limitations identified for DDTA comparison

- The study evaluates BERT-based classification, not current generative LLMs or RAG.
- The classification and TLR components are evaluated on overlapping benchmark families.
- Per-project optimized thresholds use known links and represent an optimistic upper bound.
- No independent industrial replication is reported.
- No user study measures the claimed improvement in practitioner usability.
- Review effort, confidence calibration, stale-link maintenance and explanation quality are absent.
- The method only recovers requirement-to-code links.
- Directly applying the functional filter to security analysis could discard relevant
  non-functional, user, operational or trust evidence.
- The supplementary package was not executed in this reading workflow.

## Temporal validity

- Contribution type: peer-reviewed method and two empirical studies.
- Time sensitivity: high.
- Temporal status: current as 2024 evidence for pretrained-transformer preprocessing; not current
  as a complete 2026 ranking of AI-assisted trace recovery.
- Evidence window: NoRBERT, FTLR and benchmark datasets available by RE 2024.
- Elements still applicable: task-specific filtering, unseen-project evaluation, class imbalance,
  component error propagation, comparison of automatic and gold filters, deployable versus
  optimized thresholds and public replication packages.
- Potentially obsolete elements: BERT/fastText performance, translation pipeline, selected
  competing methods and the absence of newer generative or retrieval-augmented approaches.
- Contemporary corroboration required: post-2024 LLM/RAG studies, independent reproduction and
  industrial or cross-project evaluation.
- Permitted thesis use: evidence that classification-based preprocessing can modestly improve
  FTLR on selected benchmarks and that gains are filter- and project-dependent. Do not use it
  as evidence that generative LLMs are superior or that filtering improves security analysis.

## Relationship with Documentation-Driven Threat Analysis

The source is relevant to the DDTA candidate-generation stage. It shows that preprocessing
decisions can improve or damage downstream recovery and that a pipeline must evaluate each
component separately.

### Directly supported propositions

- Requirement fragments differ in relevance to a specific trace-recovery task.
- Classification errors propagate into downstream trace recovery.
- Unseen-project evaluation is necessary when project-specific training data are unavailable.
- Class imbalance can make aggregate metrics misleading.
- Automatic filters can be compared with human labels and template rules.
- Threshold optimization and gold classification define upper bounds distinct from deployable
  performance.

### Tensions or contradictions

- The paper describes filtering as improving usability, but practitioner usability is not measured.
- The user-related filter improves some projects while reducing average performance.
- A high aggregate classification score coexists with 1.7 percent precision for a rare class.
- The best filtered approach still changes by project and has modest absolute F1.
- Optimized comparisons require known links and are not directly available in new projects.

### Research gap left open

A modern study is needed to compare BERT, embedding, RAG and generative methods under fixed
baselines, unseen projects and heterogeneous governed documents, while measuring calibration,
review effort, accepted-candidate yield and change-aware maintenance.

### Overlay-method implications

- Declare why each document fragment is relevant or irrelevant to a specific overlay task.
- Never equate `not functional` with `not security-relevant`.
- Preserve filtered fragments and the filter decision in provenance.
- Separate classifier confidence from trace-link confidence.
- Evaluate classification and downstream recovery independently and end-to-end.
- Use unseen-project and cross-project test sets.
- Report rare-class precision and recall, not only weighted aggregate scores.
- Distinguish deployable fixed thresholds from gold-optimized upper bounds.
- Measure the number of relevant fragments and links lost by filtering.
- Require human review when confidence or class support is low.
- Test filtering against security-specific ground truth before using it in threat analysis.

## Candidate thesis claims

The source can support claims that task-specific classification can improve a fine-grained
requirement-to-code recovery method on selected benchmarks, and that preprocessing errors,
class imbalance and threshold choice affect the result. It cannot support claims that
non-functional content is irrelevant to DDTA, that generative LLMs outperform classical
baselines, or that classification improves threat-model quality.
