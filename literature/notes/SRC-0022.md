## Four reading questions

### 1. Research problem and contribution

The article addresses the persistent gap between the large body of traceability tools and
techniques proposed by researchers and the limited contemporary evidence about how
practitioners actually create, manage and use trace links. It studies perceived barriers,
current practices and desired improvements, and tests whether perceptions differ between
agile and traditional development or between safety-critical and non-safety-critical systems.

The empirical contribution combines an online survey of 55 practitioners with 14
semi-structured interviews. The paper reports nine survey findings and eight interview
findings and publishes the questionnaire, interview protocol and analysis data in an online
appendix.

### 2. Starting artifacts and system representation

The study does not derive a system model. It investigates practitioner experience with
traceability across heterogeneous lifecycle artifacts and tools. Examples discussed in the
interviews include requirements, design, test cases, source code, bugs, pull requests,
commits, user stories, Jira, Trello, Bitbucket and external documentation.

The starting evidence for the research itself consists of questionnaire responses and
interview transcripts. The analysis is organized around managerial, social and technical
barriers, current practice, desired changes, development paradigm and software-system type.

### 3. Traceability, change, automation and human role

Traceability is reported as mainly manual. Practitioners frequently repurpose and customize
the tools already used for daily work instead of adopting a dedicated traceability tool.
Distributed artifacts, access restrictions and cross-tool boundaries can still create gaps;
engineers often bridge those gaps through expertise, effort and collaboration.

Automation is desired for repetitive creation and maintenance work, but interviewees do not
expect it to replace human involvement. Automatically produced links require human checking,
especially when precision and recall generate false positives or omissions. There is no
single role universally responsible for traceability; commitment, team collaboration and an
explicit agreement about responsibilities matter more than assigning one fixed owner.

### 4. Findings, limitations and relationship with DDTA / overlay

Within the survey sample, 80 percent perceived cost in money, time and effort as a main
inhibitor, but only 37 percent believed that costs outweigh benefits. Eighty-six percent
considered traceability highly important. Respondents requested better organizational
embedding through clearer benefit/cost visibility, awareness, managed practices and
communication of roles and responsibilities.

Differences between agile and traditional participants were limited. Traditional
participants rated traceability as more important and asked for more guidance, while agile
participants expressed a strong need for easier access to information sources. The study did
not find statistically significant differences between safety-critical and non-safety-critical
groups, but the authors explicitly caution against broad generalization from the sample.

For DDTA, the paper supports an approach that works inside existing development tools,
exposes tangible benefits, distributes but clarifies responsibility, and treats automation as
candidate generation followed by human review. It does not evaluate documentation-derived
threat analysis, Base Analysis, methodology overlays or security outcomes.

## Research method and corpus

- Opinion-based exploratory empirical study.
- Online questionnaire piloted with six computer-science master's students and reviewed for
  language and content.
- Thirteen current-practice and thirteen desired-change statements on Likert-type scales.
- Survey: 55 practitioners from 13 countries.
- Interviews: 14 practitioners, including eight survey respondents and six additional
  participants; approximately one hour each.
- Statistical analysis includes Pearson chi-square tests, Mann-Whitney U tests and effect
  sizes where appropriate.
- Qualitative interview analysis explains and contextualizes survey findings.
- Reusable questionnaire, interview protocol and analysis data are available on Zenodo.

## Contribution

- Contemporary practitioner evidence about traceability barriers, value and needs.
- Comparison of agile versus traditional perceptions.
- Comparison of safety-critical versus non-safety-critical perceptions.
- Explanation of why tools, automation, collaboration and organizational embedding matter.
- Reusable study instruments and underlying analysis data.

## Findings

- Expected benefit and mandate are the most common stated reasons for adoption among the
  respondents who use traceability.
- Cost is a strong inhibitor, but most respondents do not believe it exceeds the benefits.
- Traceability is considered important across the software lifecycle.
- Organizational embedding, visible return, guidance, awareness and responsibility
  communication are recurring needs.
- Distributed artifacts and tool boundaries remain difficult even when survey respondents do
  not initially classify access or tool complexity as major barriers.
- There is no universal choice between managed and ad-hoc traceability; context matters.
- Practitioners commonly repurpose existing tools and still perform substantial manual work.
- Automation is desired as assistance, not as a replacement for human verification.
- The lifecycle phase perceived as most valuable depends on the practitioner's role.

## Limitations stated by the authors

- The survey sample is 55, which limits statistical power when split into subgroups.
- Most survey respondents are in Europe, work in large companies and use agile methods.
- All interviewees work in Europe.
- Interview volunteers may introduce selection bias.
- Results are based on perceptions rather than direct observation of trace-link quality.
- Some questionnaire wording and the managerial/social/technical categorization may affect
  the results.
- The safety-critical comparison requires additional research before strong conclusions.

## Limitations identified for DDTA comparison

The study examines software traceability broadly and does not evaluate whether links are
semantically correct, versioned, stale-aware or sufficient for security analysis. It does not
measure threat coverage, model quality, analyst agreement or security-requirement quality.

## Relationship with Documentation-Driven Threat Analysis

The findings support DDTA design goals around low-friction capture, integration with normal
work, visible provenance, explicit responsibility agreements and human acceptance of
machine-produced candidates. They also warn that a technically capable traceability system
may fail if the effort and benefits are not visible to practitioners.

### Directly supported propositions

- Contemporary practitioners still report traceability as mainly manual.
- Cost is a major inhibitor, but most surveyed participants do not see it as exceeding benefits.
- Organizational and collaborative factors remain central.
- Existing work tools are often repurposed instead of replaced.
- Human checking remains expected for automated trace-link generation.

### Tensions or contradictions

- Survey responses suggest tool complexity and source access are not dominant current
  barriers, while interviews reveal important cross-tool, access-right and distributed-artifact
  problems. The authors interpret engineers as filling the gaps.
- The absence of statistically significant safety-critical differences should not be read as
  proof that criticality is irrelevant; sample size and statistical power are limited.
- The study predates the latest LLM-based tracing systems.

### Research gap left open

A method is still needed that integrates typed, versioned and reviewable trace links into
existing Git, issue-tracking and documentation workflows, measures their maintenance cost
and benefit, and evaluates correctness and staleness across realistic projects.

### Overlay-method implications

- Integrate overlay and provenance operations into tools already used by analysts and
  developers rather than requiring a separate mandatory environment.
- Produce candidates automatically, but require explicit human review for accepted links,
  findings and derived requirements.
- Provide role-oriented views without assuming one universal traceability owner.
- Record explicit responsibility agreements and review history.
- Make the benefit visible through navigation, impact analysis, stale detection and evidence
  reuse.
- Support heterogeneous artifacts such as requirements, design, tests, issues, pull requests,
  commits and external documentation.
- Keep the degree of mandatory governance configurable by project context.

## Candidate thesis claims

The source can support carefully scoped claims about practitioner perceptions in the study
sample. It cannot support universal claims that all organizations prefer repurposed tools,
that safety-criticality has no effect, or that current AI automation is ineffective.
